Yes... money.

Paper in the pocket, a vote in the ledger, and a few locomotives engraved on every note.

Paper OnlyBanknotes, called Contributes

Zaratta has no metal coins. Every payment, from a loaf of bread to a share of a railway, is made in printed paper. Citizens call each note a Contribute, a name picked with care, since what a person contributes is what earns a voice in the city. A Contribute is worth about as much as an Alfia silver piece.

Other nations find the whole arrangement absurd. Tourists arrive with bags that feel too light, tangle their mental arithmetic and spend the first week suspecting every merchant of cheating them. The city sees the same paper differently. Notes carry enormous volumes of value without weight, they move fast, and they are easy to track, which makes voting rights easy to verify.

The Muraqib catch magical and alchemical counterfeits within hours. Rumor adds that they print a few "controlled" ones themselves, to regulate circulation.

The Look of a ContributeSettlers' promises and the first railway tickets

Before Zaharât was a city it was a scatter of rail networks laid around ancient ruins, and the first paper money of the region belonged to that time. Early settlers settled large debts with promissory notes, short written contracts that spared them from hauling metal across the desert. The railways sold their own paper too, in the form of the first tickets.

When the Hukuma Central Bank replaced heavy metal coins, its designers merged the two. The note keeps the short contract wording and the signature lines of a settler's promise, and wears the borders, stubs and stamped numbers of a ticket. Each denomination also carries a cut of one of the original locomotives, engraved from the first models that ever ran on those lines.

Settlers' Promissory Notes

The oldest ancestor of the Contribute. A handwritten promise for a large exchange, honored by whoever held the paper.

The First Railway Tickets

Printed when the city was only a web of rails around the ruins. Their layout survives in every border and stub.

The Locomotive Cuts

The original locomotive models, engraved one per denomination. Historic stations, iron bridges and ornamental gears fill the margins.

The Collectors

Some collectors frame the notes and never spend them. A full set of the early series hangs in more than one railway office.

Money must flow like the desert wind, or it becomes sand.

Voting and PatronagePower measured by spending

In Zaratta political power is measured by what you spend, and holdings count for little. Frozen assets mold and rot. The city idealizes patronage, and what matters is how much of your money you spill back into its veins.

The right to vote in the Muqadimma is earned through documented circulation. Each year a citizen must prove that a minimum of value went back into the economy, through one or more of three streams. The short version of this system sits in People's Rights.

Direct Investment

Funding construction, business expansion, public works or infrastructure. The most respected stream, because it visibly improves the city.

Employment Creation

Running a business that employs others, or keeping a workshop, shop or service active. The more people employed, the louder the voice.

Consumption and Commerce

Spending on goods, services, rent and local products. The path for steady earners with no business sense.

The Threshold

One vote costs roughly six months of skilled wages, spent and documented within the year.

  • A factory supervisor might reach it through six months of actual salary.
  • A successful merchant could qualify on monthly operating expenses alone.
  • A wealthy patron can earn several votes by employing dozens and funding several projects.
  • The Vadhat family stands outside the scale. By tradition they rarely vote directly and work through their faction leaders.

The Muraqib check every claim with great care. A discrepancy suspends the vote. A fraudulent claim earns a creative punishment.

The Patronage PagesSocial currency, printed weekly

Once verified, a name is printed in Al-Kafeel. Citizens read the lists like society columns. A new name in the Grand Patron tier feeds gossip for weeks. A regular patron who disappears from the list invites speculation: bankruptcy, scandal or death. Some citizens frame their first entry the way others frame a diploma.

Most people never vote. A factory worker spends everything on rent, food and necessities. That spending circulates value and still never reaches the documentation threshold. Social rights, protections and public services belong to that worker, and a voice in governance does not.

Skilled artisans, successful shopkeepers and mid-level managers live in the frustrating gap. They earn almost enough. Some pool resources, three or four to a slot, rotating who casts the vote. The Muqadimma frowns on the practice and cannot quite outlaw it.

Where the Weight SitsThe established votes

The established voters hold the real power. They are rarely the wealthiest. A modest manufacturer who employs thirty workers and pays them fairly can outvote a miserly merchant sitting on a fortune. That produces odd alliances, with workers backing employers who circulate well over richer and stingier rivals.

The system claims to reward productivity and civic contribution over mere wealth, and technically it does. It does so often, though never every time and never evenly.

Lixander's Amal Dua'i keep pushing to lower the threshold or to add other qualification paths, such as years of residence or military service. They call it recognizing all contributors. Critics point out that it would also swell their voter base.

The Bureau of Civic ContributionWhere claims go to be stamped

Every patronage claim passes through the Bureau, and an overworked clerk stamps the forms with the rhythm of a factory machine. The Bureau also keeps exact records of every alcohol import, cocktail ingredient and establishment license, which tell more about the city's economy than most council debates.

They say the Chief Auditor, Vahira Three-Coins, is incorruptible and knows every scheme before it is finished. Her red stamp means yes and her black stamp means no. The grey stamp, UNDER REVIEW, means she knows you are lying and is letting it pass, just this once, and you had better not make her regret it.

Artistic PatronageIn Zaratta, even beauty is a transaction

Direct Investment includes a category that has grown enormously in recent decades. Funding musicians, painters, sculptors, theater troupes, public murals and monument restoration all count toward civic contribution, often at generous documented values. The city became unexpectedly lively because of it.

For artists it can feel like paradise. A talented sculptor may watch three competing patrons bid for the right to fund the next piece. Musicians perform in patron-sponsored cultural salons that double as political meetings. The Tasarue champion the scene as proof that progress does not have to be ugly, while Molochia's Taqlidi concentrate on restoration and historical preservation.

Value is the delicate point, since art valuation is subjective. The Bureau of Civic Contribution employs assessors, and a "rare technique", an "unprecedented vision" or a "cultural significance for future generations" can lift a painting far above its cost. The darker side of those numbers, with its kickbacks, phantom commissions and inflated monuments, is told in Gaming the System.
